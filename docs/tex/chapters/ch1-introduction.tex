\chapter{Introduction} \label{c1:intro}

Scientific publications accompany research, formally capturing the inputs, processing steps, insights, and outputs.
Research is rarely done by a single person in isolation; instead, new results are produced on top of previous ones.
The references between publications record this sequence, and are used to establish relationships, and to what extent any work has been useful to the rest of the scientific community.

If a publication $c$ \emph{references} another publication $p$, we say that $c$ \emph{cites} $p$, and the reference is called a \emph{citation}.

Citations form a directed relationship between papers, together they comprise a network, where nodes correspond to publications, and edges correspond to citation links.
This network is widely used to assess the impact and relevance of scientific work.

For a given publication $p$, the \emph{number of citations} it receives is the number of distinct publications that reference it.
The citation counts can be aggregated per author to estimate the influence of a researcher.

Not all citations are equally valuable.
In particular, citations can be divided into two main categories:

\begin{description}
\item
[Self-citations] where at least one author of the citing publication is also an author of the cited publication.
\item
[External citations] where there is no overlap between the authors of the citing and cited publications.
\end{description}


This distinction is important because external citations are typically considered a stronger indicator of influence, as they reflect recognition from independent researchers.
Self-citations are not inherently bad, and may occur naturally in continuous research; still, they can inflate citation-based metrics if used excessively.

The effects of self-citation go beyond the count.
Papers with more citations are easier to discover, and carry more authority with the reader.
This makes them more likely to be cited by others, even when the first citations came from the same authors~\cite{Fowler2007, R2008ImpactReinforcing}.
None of this makes self-citations wrong on their own.
For authors to build on their earlier work is a normal practice, and citing their own work is natural and expected.

When considering the citation count of an author, we want to know what part of it comes from independent publications, and what part from the same author and their collaborators.

\section{Problem statement} \label{sec:problem-statement}

The objective of this work is to create a tool that can analyze citation relationships at the author level and to distinguish between different types of citations.

Given a specific author $A$, we want to:

\begin{enumerate}
\item
identify the publications of $A$
\item
compute the total number of citations received by these publications
\item
distinguish between
    \begin{enumerate}
    \item self-citations and
    \item external citations
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

The most important (and difficult) step of the above process is the distinction between self-citations and external citations (Item 3).
In our work, we focus on the identification and classification of citations based on author overlap, providing a more clear interpretation of citation metrics.

Let us clarify our idea with the help of the example presented in Section~\ref{sec:intro-example}.

Let us consider the researchers

that had published article [GPK2007]
%~\cite{Ghinita2007}
that is described as follows.

\begin{myquote}
[GPK2007] & Gabriel Ghinita, Panos Kalnis, Spiros Skiadopoulos:
\emph{PRIVE: anonymous location-based queries in distributed mobile systems}. WWW 2007: 371-380
\end{myquote}

Panos Kalnis, a co-author of [GPK2007], later in the same year published article [KGMP2007]
%~\cite{Kalnis2007}
that cites [GPK2007] and is detailed as follows.

\begin{myquote}
[KGMP2007] & Panos Kalnis, Gabriel Ghinita, Kyriakos Mouratidis, Dimitris Papadias:
\emph{Preventing Location-Based Identity Inference in Anonymous Spatial Queries}. IEEE Trans. Knowl. Data Eng. 19(12): 1719-1733 (2007)
\end{myquote}

Using the above example, we may already determine the role of the authors in both papers.
For Panos Kalnis and Gabriel Ghinita, who authored both papers, it is clear that the citation of [GPK2007] in [KGMP2007] is a \emph{self-citation} for both.
In citing [GPK2007], they refer to their own earlier publication, and this citation does not help understand the broader perception of their work by others.

For Spiros Skiadopoulos, who only contributed to [GPK2007], determining whether the citation of [GPK2007] in [KGMP2007] is a self-citation or not is not that clear.
He did not write [KGMP2007], and had no part in the decision to cite [GPK2007]; from this perspective, the citation came from other researchers.
On the other hand, the citing article was written by two of his co-authors, who know [GPK2007] well, so the citation is not fully independent either.

Generally, considering how to classify the citation, where an author had no input presents a dilemma.
If counted as external, the citation will credit the author with recognition that came from their own collaborators.
If counted as a self-citation, it attributes to them a reference they did not make, and turns their past collaboration into a reason to discount it.
Though neither of the described outcomes reflects their actual role.
In our example, the decision to cite [GPK2007] was made by Panos Kalnis and Gabriel Ghinita, and the citation record of Spiros Skiadopoulos depends on their choice rather than on his own: he has no input in it, but has to bear the consequences.

Some would count it as his self-citation and some would not, the definitions vary across studies~\cite{Glnzel2003BIBLIOMETRICSAA, Costas2010}.
The services reporting the citations, and presenting attribution and classification of these citations, do not separate self-citations at all, while others allow excluding them without explaining which citations are removed (Section~\ref{sec:research-on-self-citation}).
As a result, the same researcher may look different depending on where the numbers come from, and the numbers taken from two services cannot be compared directly.
For the same example of Spiros Skiadopoulos, the share of self-citations among his citations ranges from 10\% in \textsf{OpenAlex} to 1.4\% in \textsf{Google Scholar}, depending only on the source of the data (Chapter~\ref{c5:evaluation}).
There are different approaches resulting in different outcomes: the citation can be classified as an \emph{external citation}, or as a lighter version of \emph{self-citation}, as the papers do share some authors, even though Skiadopoulos is not one of them.
In Section~\ref{sec:citation-classification}, we call the self-citation of Panos Kalnis and Gabriel Ghinita a \emph{direct self-citation}, and its lighter version a \emph{co-author self-citation}.

Instead of sticking to one of these approaches, we present all cases in this work.
For every author, we report the direct self-citations, the co-author self-citations and the external citations separately.
The reader can see how the citation count of the author changes under each definition, instead of relying on a single number, for which the definition is not always clear.

A formal classification of the citation types is described in Section~\ref{sec:citation-classification}.

\section{Structure of work} \label{sec:structure-of-work}

The remainder of this work is organized as follows.

\begin{description}
\item
[Chapter 2] presents related work and existing systems for citation analysis, including commonly used citation indices and data sources.

\item
[Chapter 3] describes the proposed methodology for detecting and classifying citations, including the underlying data model and processing approach.

\item
[Chapter 4] presents the implementation of the system.

\item
[Chapter 5] presents the evaluation of the system, along with illustrative results.

\item
[Chapter 6] concludes the work and discusses possible future extensions.

\end{description}
